Specifically, the research sought to determine how depletion interactions, arising from non-adsorbing polymers, influence the kinetics and morphology of phase separation and crystallization in complex fluids. By analyzing image data from NASA’s Binary Colloidal Alloy Test (BCAT-3 through BCAT-6) experiments conducted on the International Space Station, the project aimed to isolate the effects of gravity on phase behavior, quantify domain growth over multiple orders of magnitude in time and length scale, and develop a theoretical framework that connects these dynamics to hydrodynamic and thermodynamic parameters. Ultimately, this work sought to establish colloidal systems as model analogues for atomic and molecular phase transitions, providing insight relevant to both fundamental soft-matter physics and applied materials design. Approach The approach combined data-driven analysis of PSI-archived BCAT imagery with a phase-field modeling framework that coupled the Cahn–Hilliard and Stokes equations to simulate phase separation in colloid–polymer mixtures. Experimental image sequences were processed for BCAT-3, -4 and -5, using algorithms in ImageJ and MATLAB to remove background noise, enhance contrast, and compute spatial autocorrelation functions for extracting characteristic domain length scales. Theoretical modeling of BCAT-5 incorporated concentration-dependent mobility and viscosity, along with Korteweg stresses to represent interfacial tension effects, allowing for the inclusion of hydrodynamic interactions that influenced domain coarsening. The governing equations were solved using a pseudospectral semi-implicit numerical scheme, and simulation results were quantitatively compared to flight data from the BCAT experiments. Model parameters were tuned using physical data from PSI to validate predictions and refine understanding of hydrodynamic coupling, phase-domain morphology, and kinetic arrest in colloid–polymer systems. This integrated experimental–theoretical approach yielded a comprehensive, microgravity-validated description of soft-matter phase transformation mechanisms. Hypothesis Introducing non-adsorbing polymers into colloidal suspensions generates depletion-induced attractions that alter phase-transition kinetics; in microgravity, free from sedimentation and convection, these interactions can be directly modeled to reveal the intrinsic role of hydrodynamics in phase separation. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits This research provided benchmark insight into soft-matter phase transitions without gravitational interference, improving predictive capability for self-assembly, crystallization, and gelation relevant to materials manufacturing and fluids research aboard the ISS. It has also improved understanding of phase-transition kinetics and rheology in colloid–polymer mixtures, informing materials science, pharmaceutical, and chemical engineering applications, and advancing the design of next-generation coatings, gels, and nanostructured materials.
