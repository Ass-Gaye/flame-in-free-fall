A primary objective is determining the critical flow rates at which free surface destabilization (''choking'') and gas ingestion occur, emphasizing the capillary-inertial regime. The investigation will examine the relationship between critical flow rate and channel length for complete and half-channel configurations. Controlled, low-frequency flow oscillations will be introduced to probe instability onset sensitivity across geometries and lengths. High-fidelity data on free surface shapes and critical velocities will be acquired to validate theoretical models and assess underlying assumptions, refining predictive capabilities for capillary flows relevant to spacecraft fluid management. Approach The CCF experiment was operated on the ISS in the MSG. The CCF hardware consists of two experiment units, experiment unit number #1 for the parallel plate and groove geometry, and experiment unit number #2 for the wedge geometry. Initally, one experiment campaign for each experiment unit was planned. Due to the great success of the first two runs, two more additional runs were performed. The challenge for these runs was the commissioning of the fluid loop. The original design of the hardware did not foresee a commissioning from an undefined state, as it was the case after the first operation of each module. The fluid management technology builtinto the CCF hardware - however - was able to recover from an undefined fluid distribution state and allowed additional runs. Hypothesis The primary hypothesis guiding CCF-CV EU1 is that a limiting flow rate, or 'choking' phenomenon, exists in open capillary channels with parallel plate and groove geometries. A relationship between the fluid velocity, the channel geometry, and the fluid properties governs this limit. Furthermore, it is hypothesized that the introduction of wave oscillations within the flow will impact the critical flow rate, with the amplitude and frequency of these oscillations playing a significant role in the flow behavior and the onset of choking. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits The knowledge of liquid management in space is important for any device which contain liquid and gas, and have one or many free liquid surfaces. The requirements for the device which experiences different acceleration levels down to fully compensated gravity conditions (weightlessness) during its operation may include the phase separation of liquid from gas, gas from liquid, and the transport of liquid through liquid acquisition devices. The results from the CCF experiments may help to design subsystems of future spacecrafts, such as the propulsion subsystem, the thermal control subsystem and the life support subsystem for crewed exploration. The data can be used to build mathematical models which can be applied to real fluids and real dimensions. The data can be used as well to validate computational fluid dynamics software packages which are state-of-the-art tools the design of spacecrafts.<br> Capillary forces on Earth are masked by gravity, but may become dominant in small scales. This can be encountered when a wetting liquid wicks into a porous structure. It may be a wanted - as in the case of cleaning - or an unwanted - as in buildings - effect. In a wider sense the CCF experiments contribute to this topic. The main difference is that the flow was not driven by the capillary forces. Instead, the capillary forces were used to stabilize the flow path. A variety of applications on Earth may have this requirement as well. Contact(s) Michael E. Dreyeremail,Mark M.
