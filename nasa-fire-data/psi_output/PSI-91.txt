In addition, the structure and dynamics of gel-like structures formed by colloid-polymer mixtures will be measured using static and dynamic light scattering and the ultimate equilibrium structures behavior of the mixture will be determined. Finally, large scale fractal colloid aggregates and gels will be grown to measure their mechanical properties using dynamic light scattering, and potentially the cell oscillation techniques. Approach For binary colloidal crystal alloy the samples will consist of polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA) particles, stabilized by a thin coating of poly-12-hydroxystearic acid, and suspended in a mixture of decalin and tetralin adjusted to exactly match the index of refraction of the PMMA. Once in orbit, the samples will be homogenized by rapidly spinning each sample cell and suddenly stopping it. Subsequent to homogenization, the nucleation and growth of the binary colloidal alloy crystallites will be monitored by static light scatter in the forward direction. The small angle light scattering will be complemented by larger angle scattering examining the Bragg reflections of the crystals. For the colloidal-polymer mixture part of the investigation the colloidal particles will again be PMMA spheres suspended in an index matched fluid. The volume fractions of these samples will range from about 1% to 30%. The polymer will be polystyrene, with a molecular weight ranging from about 100,000 to 1,000,000. The colloid-polymer mixtures will be homogenized using the same procedure used for the binary colloid mixtures. Immediately after they are homogenized, the small angle scattering will be monitored to measure the formation of the gel-like structure. Dynamic light scattering will be performed concurrently as the gel forms to obtain a sensitive indication of the final formation of the gel. Once equilibrium is achieved, if colloidal crystals are formed in one of the phases the growth and structure of the crystals will be studied using both powder average Bragg scattering and dynamic light scattering. For the fractal colloidal aggregate and gels the aggregation of the colloidal particles will be initiated by mixing the colloidal suspension with another chemical that causes aggregation. Upon initiation of the aggregation, the growth of the colloidal aggregates will be monitored by both dynamic and static light scattering. In addition to the light scattering measurements, small amplitude oscillations of the gel structure will be performed to measure the shear modulus of the gel directly and compare with the light scattering measurements. Hypothesis The availability of microgravity will allow binary colloidal superlattices to be formed from different materials providing new insights into the nucleation and growth kinetics of binary colloidal crystal alloy and thereby establish the new field of “colloid engineering.” This work will unambiguously determine the equilibrium phase diagram for attractive colloidal particles where the attraction is induced by depletion effects through the addition of polymer. It will also provide important new insight into the transition between equilibrium and kinetically limited behavior, in the absence of sedimentation. Further, this investigation will also lead to the fabrication of fractal colloidal structures that extend over the largest range of length scales ever produced, providing a critical test of our understanding of the formation of these structures. With such extended structures, it will be possible to study their physical properties and determine the consequences of the scale invariance on the properties. It will determine the fundamental limits of the range over which fractal structures can exist. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits The long term goal of this research is to establish a new method of fabricating materials, “Colloid Engineering,” where novel materials are fabricated using colloidal particles as precursors. With appropriate choices of the sizes of the particles, it should be possible to form colloidal alloy crystals of higher volume fraction than can be made with monodisperse particles. These colloidal alloys may form the precursors for very high quality ceramics. Alternatively, the particles could be made from different materials, forming colloidal superlattices with novel properties. Then, for example, one set of particles could be plastic and the other set of particles could be a metal or a semiconductor. Alternatively, these structures may be useful in forming materials with reduced phase space available for radiation, allowing a much simpler way of fabricating structures which possess photonic band gaps. Colloid engineering may offer a simple method for making these materials that completely bypasses the difficulties faced by traditional lithography techniques. The superlattices could also be used as novel optical switches or displays. Contact(s) David A. Weitzemail,Peter N. Puseyemail,William V.
