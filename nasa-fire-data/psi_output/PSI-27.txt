The goal was to use a mixture of two different materials or binary alloys to greatly increase the flexibility of the resultant colloidal structures and qualitatively study the phase behavior and crystallization properties of these binary alloys. In addition, the investigation proposed to study the properties of mixtures of colloids and polymers where the addition of the polymer induces weak attractive interactions between the colloidal particles by the depletion mechanism, leading to a rich phase behavior for the colloidal particles. Approach BCAT-3 is implemented in a bench-top investigation which is set up on the Maintenance Work Area (MWA) on ISS. The samples are contained within a Fluids Module (FM) which held 10 samples in couvettes in individual wells. The ten samples in BCAT-3 were selected as part of three separate experiments examining different physical processes: critical point, binary alloys, and surface crystallization. The samples were manually homogenized using a stir-bar (a Teflon coated mixing magnet) inside each couvette. Samples are mixed, examined and imaged using a digital EarthKAM camera, to document the separation of colloid and liquid components and the formation of colloidal crystals over time. Hypothesis Colloidal particles would exhibit behaviors analogous to atoms, allowing the study of phase transitions, crystallization, and self-organization without the interference of sedimentation and convection present on Earth. This investigation hypothesizes that by taking advantage of microgravity and avoiding sedimentation binary alloys with very different materials and sizes and by addition of polymers particles to induce attractive forces can lead to exciting and promising form of colloidal engineering to synthesize a new class of materials. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Self-assembly of micron and submicron colloidal particles into highly ordered three-dimensional structures offers an alternative to traditional lithographic techniques, which are often limited to surface-level patterning. These ordered arrays of particles can exhibit novel optical properties, such as redirecting light via diffraction or altering local photon densities, enabling the creation of advanced optical filters, switches, and sensors. Moreover, these structures can serve as templates for macroporous materials, which have diverse chemical applications, including catalysis, separation media, and biomaterials. Precision macroporous ceramics and polymers can also be used as lightweight insulators, chromatographic materials, and catalytic supports, offering transformative solutions across industries such as energy, manufacturing, and medicine.
