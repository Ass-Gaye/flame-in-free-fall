Approach The experimental approach was to perform directional solidification experiments with a pure metal, aluminum, doped with low volume fraction of insoluble particles, ZrO2. These experiments were carried out during the Life and Microgravity Science (LMS) mission on board the space shuttle Columbia. In addition, transparent organic matrices such as succinonitrile and biphenyl doped with polystryrene particles or glass beads were used to observe the interaction between the particle and solidification interface in real time. The experiments with the organic matrices were performed during the United States Microgravity Payload 4 (USMP-4) mission also on board the space shuttle Columbia. Planar interfaces were produced through appropriate manipulation of the solidification variables, so that a clear pushing/engulfment transition (PET) of the particles could be determined. Both ground and microgravity experiments were conducted. Recognizing that the interaction between the particle and the solidification front is not completely a steady state process, a dynamic non-steady-state model was developed based on the fact that a particle, initially at rest, must have an accelerated motion in order to reach the steady-state velocity, which is the solidification velocity. To account for the solutal fields in alloys a 2D FDM fixed Cartesian grid numerical model was also developed. The models were validated using benchmark microgravity and terrestrial data. Hypothesis During solidification of metal-insoluble particles systems the particles interact with the solidification front. The solutal and thermal field, as well as fluid motion at the solid-liquid interface (SLI) will influence both interface morphology and the particle/interface interaction itself. This investigation hypothesizes that based on the nature of interaction and balance of forces acting on the particle there is a critical SLI velocity below which particles are pushed ahead of the advancing interface, and above which particle engulfment occurs. For planar SLI the critical velocity results from the forces acting on the particle. These forces include particle-SLI interaction force, drag force on the particle, gravitational force, Saffman force, and Magnus force. The last three are strongly affected by the level of gravitational acceleration. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits A fundamental understanding of the phenomena associated with particle/interface interaction during solidification will permit the development of novel techniques for processing of particulate metal matrix composites through melting and solidification. Similar concepts apply to inclusion management in casting alloys. As for most other metallurgical applications it is desired to avoid inclusion segregation at grain boundaries. Results of this research can be extrapolated straightforward to “clean steel” technology. Another interesting application of particle pushing is in the processing of single crystals ceramic where strong flux pinning particles are desired to be homogeneously incorporated in the superconducting crystal. Insights from this investigation can also be applied to the field of cryobiology where the study of the interaction of cells (yeast cells, red blood cells) with ice crystals when freezing aqueous solutions makes it possible to investigate the mechanism of the mechanical destruction of biological structures by ice crystals on freezing biological materials. In addition, this investigation can be extended to the field of frost heaving of soils that can be reduced to the behavior of soil particles (i.e. clay) in a water/ice matrix. Contact(s) Doru M.
