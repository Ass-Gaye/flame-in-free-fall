These fluids, encountered in manufacturing processes such as fiber spinning, hydraulic fracturing, and roll-to-roll film production, experience alternating regimes of shear and extension in a Lagrangian frame. By using SHERE’s microgravity dataset, the study seeks to isolate the interplay between polymer stresses, particle orientation, and flow disturbances—effects that cannot be cleanly separated in Earth’s gravity. The overarching goal is to develop quantitative predictions and mechanistic insights into how particles alter extensional flow responses, strain hardening, and energy dissipation in viscoelastic suspensions. Approach The investigation combines numerical simulations, theoretical modeling, and SHERE flight data validation. The SHERE experiments measured transient extensional responses of dilute particle suspensions in Boger fluids at moderate extension rates—regimes inaccessible on Earth due to sedimentation and buoyancy. To interpret these data, the project applies finite-difference methods in spheroidal coordinates to resolve inertia-less viscoelastic flows around suspended particles, capturing strong local stress gradients near particle surfaces. Semi-analytical theories are also employed to predict rheology in the limit of small polymer concentrations, allowing parameter sweeps that are computationally prohibitive in full simulations. Three flow classes are studied: (1) sphere suspensions in transient extension with/without pre-shear to directly compare with SHERE data; (2) spheroidal particle suspensions transitioning between shear and extension to mimic fiber-film processing; and (3) alternating shear/extension flows modeling turbulent drag reduction. Together, this multi-method approach bridges PSI experimental data with first-principles rheological predictions. Hypothesis The central hypothesis is that particle-polymer interactions fundamentally alter the transient extensional rheology of polymer solutions in ways that cannot be predicted by simple additive models of particle and polymer stresses. Specifically, the particle-induced velocity disturbances are expected to modify local polymer stress distributions, suppress strain hardening at large deformations, and alter stress development depending on flow history (e.g., pre-shear orientation effects). Furthermore, anisotropic particles such as spheroids will introduce orientation-dependent rheological signatures, leading to different stress responses under alternating shear and extension. By validating these predictions with SHERE microgravity data, the study tests whether advanced numerical and theoretical models can accurately capture the coupled dynamics of particles and polymers across relevant industrial flow regimes. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits In-Space Benefits: For space exploration and on-orbit manufacturing, understanding the rheology of particle-laden polymeric fluids is crucial for the design of efficient life-support systems, in-space fabrication, and thermal management technologies. Microgravity eliminates sedimentation, enabling stable suspensions and novel material processing methods, such as fiber spinning and advanced coatings. Insights from SHERE-based models improve predictability of fluid performance in spacecraft systems where multiphase, particle-laden fluids may be used. Earth Benefits: On Earth, particle-polymer suspensions underpin industries such as hydraulic fracturing, fiber spinning, extrusion molding, roll-to-roll manufacturing, and enhanced oil recovery. Adding particles imparts tailored mechanical or conductive properties but also increases energy demands due to altered rheology. This project provides mechanistic predictions for how particle orientation and polymer stress coupling influence flow resistance, helping optimize industrial processes, reduce energy consumption, and enable development of high-performance materials. By combining SHERE microgravity data with advanced modeling, this work addresses gaps in extensional rheology knowledge that hinder efficient design of complex fluid processing systems.
