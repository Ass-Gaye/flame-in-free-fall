Specific objectives were: (1) Identify the laminar smoke-point properties of non-buoyant jet diffusion flames under various co-flow conditions in microgravity. (2) Determine the effects of burner diameter and co-flow velocity on the luminous flame lengths and smoke points. (3) Quantify the sooting propensity of different fuels, such as ethylene, propane, and propylene mixtures. (4) Validate the correlation between luminous flame shape and residence time under microgravity conditions. (5) Investigate the impact of air and fuel flow rate variations on smoke point phenomena in space. Approach The experimental setup in the microgravity science glovebox consisted of an apparatus with a flow duct with integrated nozzles, cameras, and fuel delivery systems. Fuels tested included methane, ethylene, propane, propylene, and propylene mixtures. Each flame was ignited in a controlled co-flow air environment, with burner diameters of 0.41 mm, 0.76 mm, and 1.6 mm and co-flow velocities ranging from 5.4 cm/s to 65 cm/s. Fuel flow rates were adjusted to identify the smoke point, marked by the flame's transition to emitting soot. Data such as flame lengths were recorded using still and video cameras. Measurements included flame height, radiative heat losses, and co-flow air velocity distributions. In total 526 flames were studied. Hypothesis The research hypothesizes that by minimizing gravity-driven transport phenomena scientists can study the laminar smoke-point properties of non-buoyant jet diffusion flames. Flame properties are expected to differ significantly from those observed under normal gravity. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits (1) Will improve our understanding of soot formation mechanisms and help design safer spacecraft environments. (2) Will contribute to refining our computational models for predicting flame behaviors under non-buoyant conditions. (3) Will enhance our knowledge of soot generation (4) Could possibly provide insights to better fuel formulations and combustion processes.
