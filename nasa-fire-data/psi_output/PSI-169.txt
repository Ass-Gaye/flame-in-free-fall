In NPH, highly charged nanoparticles are used to stabilize much larger (by 2 orders of magnitude or more), non-charged particles. To accomplish this stabilization, the volume fraction of nanoparticles within the suspension need only be on the order of 1e-5 up to 1e-4 to stabilize at 1 to 2 vol% suspension of the larger particles. The objective of this work is to gain fundamental insight into the physics of NPH with the aim of addressing the following unresolved questions: • How will NPH suspensions behave in thermal or electrical field gradients as they do under gravity? • How does the halo form or re-form if disturbed? • Can NPH (and manipulation thereof) be used to assemble and reconfigure colloidal crystals? • How does the nanoparticle charge impact halo formation? Approach The ACE-T12 experiment utilizes the Fluids Integrated Rack (FIR) and the Light Microscopy Module (LMM). The FIR is built to accommodate a wide range of experiments. Accordingly, the ACE-T12 experiment included confocal microscopy, which used a 532-nm frequency-doubled Nd:YAG laser, a confocal scanner, and an 8- to 12-bit digital CCD camera on a Leica DM-RXA microscope. The scanner captured 30 frames per second of confocal images by the CCD camera. ACE-T12 utilizes capillary cells. Three capillary cells with different concentrations of nanoparticles were provided in a sample module. Here is a brief high-level descriptive outline of the investigation on-orbit procedures that gives general experiment steps for each capillary. 1- Short mix of sample. 2- Ramp temperature to find critical temperature and reduce temperature and initiate a very long mix of sample. 3- Determine Region of Interest (ROI). 4- Ramp temperature continuously while imaging. 5- Mix sample followed by Single temperature step visits. 6- Mix sample followed by stepwise temperature ramp with image stacks. Hypothesis When NPH suspensions were allowed to settle under gravity, those that were stabilized (while in suspension) formed highly ordered, 3-dimensional colloidal crystal structures. Current theory suggests that nanoparticles create a charge layer by forming a cage, or Halo, around the larger particles. Another hypothesis regarding the NPH stabilization mechanism focuses entirely on nanoparticle adsorption to the surface of the larger particle. Based on the measurement of the interaction forces between two larger colloidal surfaces in a nanoparticle suspension, it was found that the force curves obtained could be fitted using a model based on nanoparticle adsorption. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Ultimately, the ability to design colloidal particles with a variety of well-controlled three-dimensional bonding symmetries opens a wide spectrum of new structures for colloidal self-assembly, beyond particle assemblies whose structures are defined primarily by repulsive interactions and shape. Such materials might include photonic crystals with programmed distributions of defects. Optical technology utilizing such materials may offer intriguing solutions to unavoidable heat generation and bandwidth limitations facing the computer industry.
