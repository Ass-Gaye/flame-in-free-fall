These quantum sensors are particularly relevant for fundamental physics research, with proposals including unprecedented tests of the validity of the weak equivalence principle, precision measurements of the fine structure and gravitational constants, and detection of gravity waves and dark matter/dark energy. This investigation will utilize the capabilities of NASA's multi-user Cold Atom Laboratory (CAL), in the microgravity environment onboard the International Space Station (ISS), to study mitigation schemes for the leading-order systematics expected to limit future high-precision measurements of fundamental physics with AIs in microgravity. The flight experiments, supported by theoretical investigations and ground studies at Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL), will concentrate on the physics of pairwise interactions and molecular dynamics in ultracold quantum gases as a means to overcome uncontrolled AI shifts associated with the gravity gradient and few-particle collisions. A space-based dual-species AI will be further developed and used, which is planned as an on-orbit upgrade to the CAL science module after approximately a year of science investigations, for proof-of-principle tests of systematic mitigations and phase-readout techniques for use in the next-generation of precision metrology experiments based on AIs in microgravity. The proposed studies require the effective position invariance, long free fall times, and extremely low temperature samples uniquely available with the CAL apparatus. It is anticipated that these studies can lead to the unprecedented level of control and accuracy necessary for AIs to explore some of the most fundamental physical concepts in nature. Approach Investigation comprises two main aspects: the creation of ultracold atomic gases and the subsequent manipulation and probing of these gases to study systematic mitigations. For dual-species operations the process is slightly more complicated, especially with the addition of a sympathetic cooling step, however, the general process is fairly standardized. Creation of Ultracold Atomic Gas: -Laser Cooling: Atoms are released into a vacuum chamber and collected into a magneto-optical trap (MOT), where they are initially cooled using laser cooling techniques. This technique cools the sample by essentially ‘pushing’ the laser against the motion of the atoms effectively lowering the momentum, and therefore the velocity, kinetic energy, and temperature, of the sample. This is followed by brief stages of “compressed MOT”, in which the trapping force of the MOT is increased to compress the gas, and then an “optical molasses” stage, during which the quadrupole magnetic field is turned off and the laser frequency is further detuned to minimize the temperature of the gases. -State Preparation: The sample is optically pumped to a low magnetic field-seeking quantum state. The atoms in this state are then subjected to an intermediate quadrupole magnetic field which traps the atoms from the MOT and allows for the transfer from the MOT, located at the bottom of the science region, to an atom-chip-based trap, located at the top of the science region. -Evaporative Cooling: The atoms undergo forced evaporative cooling, using frequency sweeps of either RF or microwave fields, to bring the gases below the critical temperature, leading to the formation of a Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC). Evaporative cooling cools the sample by removing the highest-energy atom from the trap so that the remaining atoms re-thermalize at progressively lower temperatures. -Decompression and Release: The tightness of the atom trap is adiabatically relaxed to further cool the atoms via decompression colling. After a short hold time in the relaxed trap, the atoms are released by rapidly turning the atom-chip and bias coil currents to zero to allow the atoms to expand in freefall. At this point, investigators can search for the primary signatures of a BECs for confirmation that one was formed. Formation of a BEC is typically associated with the behavior of the velocity distribution, such as the presence of a narrow peak centered at near zero velocity and the low velocity peak growing (greater fraction of atoms in this domain) as the temperature decreases. Manipulation and Probing: -Probing Techniques: The ultracold gas is probed in freefall using laser, magnetic field, radiofrequency, and/or microwave pulses. These techniques are designed to establish atoms in the proper state, perform atom interferometry, or establish and investigate the pairwise interactions and molecular dynamics crucial for mitigating systematics in high-precision measurements. -Time-of-Flight Imaging: After a predetermined time of flight, the sample is imaged using absorption imaging techniques. This process involves shining resonant light on the atoms and taking two images, per atomic species, of the shadow cast in the light: one of the sample and a reference image post-destruction of the sample. As implied, this is the end of the experimental cycle for this specific BEC as the measurement process destroys the sample. This approach is particularly suited for studying mitigation schemes for leading-order systematics that can affect future high-precision measurements with atom interferometers (AIs) in microgravity. By leveraging the unique microgravity environment provided by NASA’s Cold Atom Laboratory (CAL) on the International Space Station, this investigation aims to achieve the level of control and accuracy required for groundbreaking tests of fundamental physics principles, such as the weak equivalence principle, and precision measurements of the fine structure and gravitational constants. The integration of theoretical investigations and ground studies at JPL supports these flight experiments, enabling comprehensive understanding and development of systematic mitigation techniques. Hypothesis This investigation hypothesizes that the control of interactions and association/dissociation of weakly-bound molecules in ultracold quantum gases can overcome uncontrolled Atomic Interference (AI) shifts in space which are caused by the gravity gradient and few-particle collisions. Investigators will interrogate this hypothesis by conducting tests using ultracold quantum gases and exploring Feshbach resonances at new energy regimes. Investigators expect unprecedented levels of control and accuracy in the AI that will enable exploration of some of the most fundamental physical concepts in nature and open up venues for exploration of novel quantum phases in exotic dynamical regimes. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Our studies are designed to achieve technological advances in precision metrology that can only be realized in the microgravity environment of the Cold Atom Laboratory (CAL). The tools of ultracold atomic and molecular physics are utilized (namely Feshbach resonances) for exquisite control of the differential center-of-mass distributions of the dual-species quantum gases and on methods to use the fundamentals of few-body interactions to maintain coherence in atomic ensembles for enhanced precision sensor capabilities. Subsequent proof-of-principle studies with the dual-species atom interferometer on CAL will further advance the state of the art for precision interferometry with ultracold matter waves. The impact of such research to the field of metrology can be seen through its potential to increase precision for atom-interferometry and also the possibility of engineering highly efficient system-specific devices based on the fundamental nature of few-body interactions. The microgravity environment of the CAL facility strongly favors such explorations and allow for the possibility of uncovering novel effects and quantum phases of matter, a major goal in ultracold quantum gases and other disciplines of fundamental physics. These studies can benefit life on Earth by providing both fundamental understanding of nature in previously inaccessible environments and energy regimes, and by enhancing the tools available for scientific exploration at the highest precision.
