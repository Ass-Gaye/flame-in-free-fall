CSLM experimental data are stored in NASA’s Physical Sciences Informatics (PSI). Microgravity experiments offer a unique opportunity to test existing numerical modeling and theories of phase coarsening. The objective of this research is to test existing numerical modeling methods for simulation of the microstructure evolution that occurs during phase coarsening, using experimental data for Sn-Pb mixtures currently held in PSI. Results from this research can be found in the following publications: Wang, K.G., Glicksman, M.E. Testing Numerical Modeling of Phase Coarsening by Microgravity Experiments. JOM 69, 2825–2830 (2017). https://doi.org/10.1007/s11837-017-2553-x Approach (1) use data archived in NASA's PSI system from the Coarsening in Solid-Liquid Mixtures (CSLM) microgravity experiments to determine mean and maximum particle sizes in each sample processed through the CSLM microgravity experiments; (2) apply a two-dimensional (2-D) phase-field simulation code to allow simulation and evolution of initial microstructures that were observed in the CSLM experiments for Sn-Pb solid-liquid phase mixtures, and which provide input data for our phase-field code to numerically evolve coarsened microstructures over time; (3) calculate particle size distributions (PSDs) from the prior NASA CSLM experiments, our phase-field and multiparticle diffusion simulations, and from diffusion screening theory. Hypothesis Phase-field and multiparticle diffusion numerical methods can be applied to simulate the evolution of the microstructure during phase coarsening, delivering accurate statistical physical quantities such as the particle size distribution (PSD). Phase-field modeling can adequately capture the complexity of the microstructures developed during phase coarsening at large volume fraction. In contrast, multiparticle diffusion modeling can track the evolution of microstructures consisting of nearly spherical particles that develop during phase coarsening at lower volume fraction. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits The contributions from our research activities improved the understanding of fundamental materials phenomena, i.e., phase coarsening. We advanced the state of the art in both theory and simulation. Better understanding of two-phase microstructures will help engineers improve process designs in economically important areas, including liquid-phase sintering for the production of hard tool materials. The theory and simulation tools developed herein that are tested by microgravity experiments, will provide technical pointers for the improvement of important processes, microstructures, and materials properties. The improved knowledge of microstructure evolution in this project will have significant impact on alloy design and service-life strategies for alloy materials. The theory and simulation tools developed will provide guidance toward exploring the structure and properties of alloys. Finally, the theoretical and computational tools will save money and time for alloy modification.
