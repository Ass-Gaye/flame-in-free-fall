The scientific objectives of the second flight of the IDGE was to: characterize the three-dimensional shape of a dendrite tip; accumulate a dense data set on dendritic velocities and radii in the diffusion-limited infinite boundary regime; and to form dendritic velocity data sets to clarify issues in the literature about whether convection or near-field boundary conditions affect the dendritic growth measurements in microgravity in the lower supercooling range. Approach The sample material and temperature controls were identical to the USMP-2 investigation. In addition to the high-resolution film data collected after the shuttle landed, growth velocities were measured in near-real time by downlinked, binary CCD images. Many growths were measured from CCD images alone and these steady-state velocity measurements were accurate to 2%. Hypothesis The hypothesis of this investigation is that as a function of the supercooling there exists an explicit dendritic dynamic operating state defined by dendritic tip velocity, V and the dendrite tip paraboloidal radius of curvature, R. The Peclet number obtained from the Ivantsov solution for each specified supercooling yielded an infinite number of solutions for V and R. Therefore it is hypothesized that a second independent equation or length scale exists, which, when combined with the Ivantsov diffusion solution, will mathematically select the observed unique dynamic operating state. Two of the candidates for the additional crystal growth physics that could provide the second crucial equation are the effects from the solid-melt interfacial energy, the influence of temperature fluctuations in the melt, or both. Aside from the original hypothesis for USMP-2, the USMP-3 also tested the hypothesis that both the magnitude of the microgravity level and the growth angle with respect to the quasi-static microgravity vector would affect the growth velocity. With additional and sufficient data from USMP-3 it was anticipated to construct a three-dimensional reconstruction of the non-parabolic, non-body-of-revolution dendritic tip shape. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits The size, shape, spacing, and orientation of the dendrites in cast metal alloys have a major effect on the strength, ductility and toughness. Nearly all cast-metal alloys used in everyday products, such as automobiles and airplanes, are composed of dendritic microstructures. Metallurgists have long sought to predict and control alloy microstructures. The development of automated, cost effective manufacturing techniques ultimately depends on the precision with which the problem in non-equilibrium pattern formation can be solved. In principle, the intent is to incorporate fundamental understanding of microstructures into computer codes that simultaneously help design materials with made to order properties and optimize their manufacturability and performance. Dendritic growth experiments conducted in the microgravity environment can provide benchmark data to validate such predictive models.
