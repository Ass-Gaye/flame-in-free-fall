By performing solidification experiments both on earth and in a microgravity environment, the effects of gravity-driven melt convection and sedimentation/floatation of unattached dendrite fragments and equiaxed crystals on the CET will be examined. The origin of equiaxed grains in non-refined alloys is of particular interest in the proposed experiments. The benchmark data from these experiments can be used to test theoretical models and computer simulations. Approach Experiments were conducted in the Solidification Using a Baffle in Sealed Ampoules (SUBSA) furnace to investigate the CET for stationary samples without an imposed temperature gradient. Most experiments with aluminum-copper (Al-Cu) alloys, namely, Al-4wt%Cu, Al-10wt%Cu and Al-18wt%Cu alloys. The reason is that Al-Cu alloys are better suited for the duplicate ground-based experiments. If solidified upward (low temperature at the bottom, high at the top), thermosolutal natural convection of the melt will generally be minimized. The sedimentation or floatation of dendrite fragments and small equiaxed grains can be controlled by the solute concentration. For smaller solute concentrations, the solid is heavier than the melt and will sediment, while for higher solute concentrations the solid is lighter and will float. Hence, dendrite fragments and equiaxed grains in the Al-18wt%Cu alloy will float upwards ahead of the columnar front, and a CET can be expected. On the other hand, for the Al-4wt%Cu alloy, the fragments will sediment and remain between the primary columnar trunks, and a CET may not occur. For Al-10wt%Cu alloys, the solid is approximately neutrally buoyant in the melt. An additional experiment with an unrefined Al-7wt%Si alloy was also conducted. No grain refiner was used. In all proposed CET experiments, the samples were first fully melted and then solidified by decreasing the temperature of the SUBSA furnace at a controlled rate. The samples were not moved, and no temperature gradient were explicitly imposed. Corresponding flight and terrestrial experiments were conducted. Samples were analyzed by measuring the grain structure and any macrosegregation. Hypothesis The primary hypothesis of this investigation is that fragmentation of columnar dendrites can lead to a CET in non-grain-refined alloys. The secondary dendrite arms that detach from the primary columnar trunks are a potent source of new (equiaxed) grains. However, for a CET to occur the growth of the primary columnar trunks must be blocked by equiaxed grains. This can only be accomplished if the secondary dendrite fragments are transported to the columnar front, rather than remain between the primary columnar trunks. Due to natural convection of the melt and sedimentation or floatation of the fragments, such transport can be expected to readily occur in the gravitational field of earth. In the absence of gravity, it is not clear if fragmentation alone, without fragment transport, is sufficient to cause a CET. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits The CET refers to the transition between the elongated grains in the outer portions of a casting and the more rounded grains in the center. Understanding this transition is fundamental to determining what type of grain structure forms in castings of most metal alloys (steel, aluminum, copper, etc.). Often, a fully equiaxed structure is preferred, but the fully columnar structures of many turbine blades are an important exception. In addition to its high practical significance, the CET represents a “holy grail” in the area of modeling and simulation of casting. This is because in order to realistically predict the CET, almost every physical phenomenon at every length scale must be taken into account simultaneously. This investigation will not only provide an improved understanding of the CET, but also models and computer simulations of the grain structure formation in metal castings that can be used by industry to better industry to better understand and optimize their casting processes.
