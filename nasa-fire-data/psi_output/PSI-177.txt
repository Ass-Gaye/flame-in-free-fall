Three specific objectives include: (1) investigation of phase separation kinetics of anisotropic particles in toggled fields, (2) study the effect of toggle duty-cycle on the phase diagram, and (3) perform concurrent ground-based self-assembly experiments using the same magnetic colloids and extend the work to dielectric (insulating) particles driven by toggled alternating current (AC) electric fields.Concurrent ground-based experiments help translate International Space Station (ISS) discoveries to Earth-based manufacture by providing additional insights into the structure of equilibrated phases. Approach This ISS investigation was conducted using magnetic prolate ellipsoids with aspect ratios ranging from 2:1 to 4:1. Experiments involving spheres was also be conducted for comparison purposes. The sample material consisted of randomly oriented nano Fe3O4 coated polymer beads within a fluid filled vial, creating a magnetorheological fluid (MR). The vials were subjected to a pulsed magnetic field, which created a configuration of three-dimensional structures.The particle orientation was determined from birefringence measurements using crossed polarizers. The effects of particle shape, anisotropy, and magnetic field toggling frequency on the phase separation of particles was examined. Relatively short duration, ground-based experiments was conducted using the same magnetic particles to provide additional details about structure formation from particle-level to mesoscale structure. Hypothesis Due to their anisotropic dynamics and interactions, investigators hypothesize that particle shape, in particular anisotropy (having different physical properties along different molecular axes), affects the phase separation kinetics of suspensions in toggled fields. Theoretical and simulation results suggesting that dynamic self-assembly can access a broad range of states analogous to equilibrium phases, even metastable ones, was also tested. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Discoveries from this work could lead to advanced materials for space applications such as better thermal shields, protection from micrometeorites, materials for energy production, energy-transfer devices, control systems and new mechanical actuators and sensors for robotic and human missions. This investigation helps advance the manufacture of next-generation nanomaterials for applications on Earth such as thermal shields, sound damping devices, shock absorbers, camouflage, medical diagnostic and environmental sensors, display technologies and advanced coatings. The technology also is expanding into larger-scale applications such as building foundation stabilizers for areas prone to earthquakes.
