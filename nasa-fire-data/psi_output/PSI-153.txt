Specific questions to be answered were: (a) How does processing in microgravity change the composition of the matrix of the glass-forming alloys compared to processing in gravity? (b) How does the volume fraction of the W spheres affect the composition and properties of the matrix? (c) How does the composition and viscosity of the glass-forming alloy affect the diffusion of W in microgravity? (d) How do the different diameter of W spheres affect the diffusion of W into the matrix? (e) Does the dissolved W provide a mechanism to improve the wear resistance and toughness of the matrix by providing reinforcement for brittle phases? Approach The objective of the flight experiment is to process 12 samples of two glass-forming alloys (Vitreloy 106 and Zr48Cu47.5Al4Co0.5) in microgravity with three different volume fractions of W spheres (5%, 15% and 30%) with two different diameters of W spheres (25-36 μm diameter and 140-150 μm diameter). The cast composite samples were 15 mm in diameter and 12 mm long cylinders. The W particles were distributed as homogeneously as possible without sedimentation. On orbit, the samples were loaded into the Low Gradient Furnace (LGF) on the Materials Science Research Rack. They were held for 120 minutes at 1,200 °C (1,473 K) in microgravity with the intent to dissolve the W particles into the matrix and to create a fine distribution of W dendrites homogenously distributed throughout. The samples were then cooled back to room temperature slowly, solidifying the morphology and composition. The Vitreloy 106 samples were compared with Zr48Cu47.5Al4Co0.5 in terms of composition of the matrix, morphology of the W dendrites, and mechanical properties, with comparisons drawn between samples produced on-orbit and on the ground to distinguish the effects of microgravity. Hypothesis The hypothesis for the experiment is that microgravity promotes composite formation by stabilizing the interface between the W nano-particle nuclei and the bulk liquid. In contrast, on Earth, the heavy W particles would fall out of the system as they form. Therefore, the composition of the solid formed on Earth will differ from that formed in microgravity. On Earth, the samples should solidify in a manner consistent with the Scheil solidification approximation meaning: (a) liquid diffusion is instant over experimental timescales (b) solid-state diffusion does not occur (solid falls out of the system as it forms) (c) the solid composition profile will follow the solidus as the system cools, until all the liquid is consumed. In contrast, in microgravity, the solid/liquid interface lifetime is much longer, so there is more opportunity for solid back-diffusion. For this case, the composition profile will approach (but not reach) that of the equilibrium solidification case. After processing in both gravity and microgravity, experimental compositional data at the matrix-sphere interface will be obtained. It is expected that composites formed on Earth will solidify consistent with Scheil solidification and composites formed in microgravity will deviate from Scheil, approaching equilibrium solidification. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits The development of innovative new materials is a fundamental part of future terrestrial and space technologies. These materials have a tremendous benefit towards improving life on Earth, from improving the accessibility and cost of solar power, to making cars safer and stronger, to reducing the mass of aircraft, for example. Innovative technologies typically require new materials tailored for those applications. This drives materials development.
