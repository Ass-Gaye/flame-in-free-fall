An objective was to determine the true three-dimensional low-energy (equilibrium) structure of an magneto-rheological (MR) emulsion in a pulsed magnetic field. InSPACE-1 aimed to conduct a microscopic video study of the MR fluid in a pulsed magnetic field to determine the effect of varying magnetic field, pulse frequency, and particle size on the equilibrium microstructures. A pulsed magnetic field is used to mimic the forces applied to these fluids in real applications, such as vibration damping systems. A pulsed field also tends to produce intricate, thick structures with different properties than structures produced by a constant magnetic field. Approach The MR samples for InSPACE-1 were ferrofluid emulsions consisting of iron oxide nanoparticles suspended in a solution of sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, an anionic surfactant), and ultrapure water. These samples were comprised of uniform particles sizes of 0.31, 0.40, and 0.66 μm and volume fraction 2 percent. The parameters varied during the series of tests were the field strength, the particle size, and the pulse frequency. During experimental runs the Lightbox Assembly provided the backlighting source to illuminate the samples. The InSPACE Avionics Assembly was used to output an electrical current and frequency that could be adjusted to produce a pulsed-magnetic field inside the Helmholtz coil. This magnetic field caused the particles in the fluid to group together, or aggregate, and form microstructures inside the fluid. A camera was used to record the display on the avionic assembly. The Optics Assembly was composed of two cameras with optic lens for a right angle view and a straight view of the sample. A Helmholtz coil was mounted on the optic assembly and housed the InSPACE-1 sample. For InSPACE-1, the Helmholtz Coil Assembly (CA) and the sample Vial Assemblies were integrated as a single unit. Hypothesis The primary hypothesis is that energy barriers preventing colloidal phase transition can be overcome by changing the magnetic driving frequency and forces. In these gel systems, gravity plays a dominant role and would slowly compress and deform the gel structures when similar experiments are performed on Earth, whereas in space these structures can be maintained as long as the magnetic forces are applied. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits MR fluids can quickly transition into a nearly solid-like state when exposed to a magnetic field and return to their original liquid state when the magnetic field is removed. Their relative stiffness can be controlled by adjusting the strength of the magnetic field. Due to the rapid-response interface that they provide between mechanical components and electronic controls, for terrestrial applications MR fluids can be used to improve or develop new brake systems, seat suspensions, robotics, clutches, airplane landing gear, and active damping systems for bridges (to counter act wind gusts) and buildings. For space exploration the results will lead to MR applications for limb and dextrous motion in robotic components and human-robotic interfaces for EVA suits. Contact(s) Alice P. Gastemail,Robert D.
