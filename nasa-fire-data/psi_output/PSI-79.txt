On the ISS and concurrently in earth control experiments, compare incorporation of protein aggregates (monomers versus dimers, trimers, etc.) into growing protein crystals. Detection of aggregates in the crystal will be qualitatively assessed via LMM’s and laboratory fluorescence confocal microscopes and quantitatively measured using mass spectrometry (ion spray and/or MALDI). Based on the percentage incorporation of larger aggregates within the crystal, the effect of molecular filtering based on differences in diffusion rates will be assessed. 2. Measure crystal growth rates in 1g versus μg for different size aggregates of proteins with molecular weights ranging from less than 20kDa to more than 1,200kDa. 3. Compare the defect density and crystal quality via mass spectroscopy and x-ray diffraction quality of crystals grown at different rates in a 1g environment. Approach A total of four cassettes were sent to the ISS, containing eight samples each. Each sample could contain either; (1) Lysozyme, (2) pfGST, (3) BSA, or (4) XptA1. For each protein investigated, preparative size exclusion chromatography (SEC) was used to fractionate protein monomers from the naturally occurring protein aggregates. The aggregates were then segmented into small, medium, and large categories. Fluorophores (excitation wavelengths: 594 and 488) were chosen and attached to the macromolecules, producing a bright fluorescent signal (red and green) and each exhibited a slow photo-bleach so that fluorescence was maintained for the duration of the proposed crystallization experiments. The samples were upmassed frozen at -80C and thawed to ambient temperature (~20C). After thawing nucleation of the proteins were observable within 24 – 96 hours. Observations were conducted with a confocal fluorescent microscope where the total fluorescence observed for each crystal (after normalizing for the different crystal volumes) provides an estimation of the relative amount(s) of aggregated (containing the fluorophore) versus monomeric (no fluorophore) protein incorporated within the growing crystal. Hypothesis This investigation addresses the hypothesis that improved quality of microgravity-grown protein crystals is the result of two macromolecular characteristics that exist in a buoyancy-free, diffusion-dominated solution: • Slower crystal growth rates, due to slower protein transport to the growing crystal surface • Predilection of growing crystals to incorporate protein monomers versus higher protein aggregates due to differences in transport rates Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Using three-dimensional protein structure information, researchers can determine how proteins function and in cases where these proteins are involved in disease processes, the structure is often used to design new drugs that specifically interact with the protein. Many leading drugs today are the product of structure-based drug design. While enormous strides have been made in the last decade, there remain a large number of important proteins where the difficulty of obtaining crystals of sufficient quality is the chief barrier to their structural analysis. The impact of this investigation is for one important class of proteins, membrane proteins, comprises a number of targets identified by the pharmaceutical industry as high-value commercial opportunities (membrane proteins were the targets for approximately 67% of all past marketed drugs and it is estimated that they will comprise targets for an equal percentage of all future drugs). Membrane protein crystallization represents one area where the onset of space commercialization can maximize the impact of the microgravity environment in space-based research applications for the academic, government and pharmaceutical industries. Other areas include high-value aqueous proteins and protein complexes (based on their functional importance in biological systems) many of which yield crystals of poor quality under 1g laboratory conditions. Access to unique data optimized in microgravity could have great relevance for understanding protein structures and advancing new drugs into the pharmaceutical market.
