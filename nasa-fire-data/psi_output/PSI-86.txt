Liquid crystals are used for display screens in televisions and clocks, and they also occur in soaps and in cell membranes. The experiment allows detailed studies of the behavior of these structures, and how microgravity affects their unique ability to act like both a liquid and a solid crystal. The specific research objectives are: -To exploit the unique characteristics of freely suspended liquid crystals in a microgravity environment to advance the understanding of fluid state physics. -Study basic 2D hydrodynamics/fluid physics, probe droplet/island diffusion, hydrodynamic interactions, and droplet/island coalescence Approach The OASIS experiment’s goal is to better understand the unique behavior of liquid crystals in microgravity. OASIS observes the various aspects of liquid crystals behavior in microgravity such as overall fluid motion, diffusion (the movement of particles from an area of high concentration to low concentration) and the merging of liquid crystal film layers called islands, Fundamental studies of complex fluid physics behavior will be accomplished in two dimensional fluids using freely suspended liquid crystals. The impact of the research is to advance the understanding of two-dimensional system physics of complex fluids. Space helmets could use ferroelectric liquid crystal micro-displays. It could advance the understanding of well aligned very high speed electro-optic devices. Hypothesis In a microgravity environment, freely suspended smectic liquid crystal (FSLC) films will exhibit unique hydrodynamic and interfacial behaviors, such as reduced coarsening rates of island emulsions, enhanced 2D thermocapillarity effects, and distinct equilibrium spatial organization of islands, due to the absence of gravitational forces, convection, and sedimentation, enabling precise tests of theoretical models of 2D hydrodynamics and phase behavior. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Future space helmets may use certain types of liquid crystals in small display screens directly applied to the face shield where astronauts can easily view them. Understanding how liquid crystals behave in microgravity helps researchers to design liquid crystal displays (LCDs) that can perform better in space. Liquid crystals can flow like a liquid, but contain molecules that are arranged in a specific pattern, just like a crystal. Greater understanding of the physics behind these structures could lead to improved liquid crystal display devices, including devices with improved color contrast and response times. It could also advance research in high-speed electro-optic devices used to control a light beam. Contact(s) Joseph Maclennanemail,Noel A.
