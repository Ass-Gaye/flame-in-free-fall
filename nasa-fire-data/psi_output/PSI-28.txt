The BCAT-4 investigation had two primary objectives corresponding to specific samples: Critical Point (BCAT-3-4-CP): Depending on their relative distances and energies with respect one another, atoms and molecules organize themselves to form gases, liquids, or solids. By varying the concentrations of the microscale spheres, this experiment studied the critical point of these systems where gases and liquids no longer exist as separate entities and a new state of matter forms when one reaches a critical point. Polydispersion (BCAT-4-Poly): BCAT-4-Poly will use model hard-spheres (suspended in a liquid that makes them transparent) to explore pure and seeded colloidal crystal nucleation and the effects of polydispersity, providing insight into how to understand and control the processes used by nature to bring order out of disorder. Approach BCAT is implemented in a bench-top investigation which is set up on the Maintanence Work Area (MWA) on ISS. The first set of samples was composed of mixtures of colloids and polymers near their liquid-gas critical point. The second set consisted of spherical colloidal particles with effective volume fractions resulting in an amorphous or glassy phase in normal gravity. The samples are contained within a Fluids Module (FM) which held 10 samples in couvettes in individual wells. The samples were manually homogenized using a stirbar (a teflon coated mixing magnet) inside each couvette. Samples are mixed, examined and imaged using a digital EarthKAM camera to document the separation of colloid and liquid components and the formation of colloidal crystals over time. Hypothesis Colloidal particles would exhibit behaviors analogous to atoms, allowing the study of phase transitions, crystallization, and self-organization without the interference of sedimentation and convection present on Earth. This investigation hypothesizes that by taking advantage of microgravity and avoiding sedimentation binary alloys with very different materials and sizes and by addition of polymers particles to induce attractive forces can lead to exciting and promising form of colloidal engineering to synthesize a new class of materials. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits The findings of BCAT-4 can directly contribute to the development of advanced materials for use in space missions, such as lightweight and durable composites for spacecraft or self-assembling structures for space habitats. Additionally, understanding colloidal behavior aids in the optimization of life support systems, such as those used for water recycling and air purification, which are vital for sustaining long-duration missions to destinations like Mars. On Earth, the results of BCAT-4 have practical applications across multiple industries, including consumer goods, medicine, and manufacturing. For instance, the study of colloidal stability and crystallization informs the design of more stable food products, cosmetics, and pharmaceutical formulations. The findings also enhance industrial processes like 3D printing, where precise control of particle interactions is crucial for producing high-quality materials. Furthermore, BCAT-4 contributes to advancements in medicine by improving drug delivery systems that rely on colloidal suspensions, ensuring more effective treatments. By expanding the understanding of fundamental physical processes, BCAT-4 drives innovation in material design and production, benefiting diverse sectors and improving everyday products and technologies.
