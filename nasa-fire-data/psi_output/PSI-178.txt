These property measurements were used to establish correlations with atomic structure and glass formation. The research exploits the unique capabilities in microgravity to measure the thermophysical properties – density, thermal expansion coefficient, viscosity and surface tension – over a wide temperature range. Samples, including glasses, were recovered after melt processing in microgravity for ground-based analysis and comparison with materials made on Earth. The work underpins development of a materials property-structure data set that can help in development of new materials and in meeting requirements of material utilization, processing in space, and exploration. Approach Density, thermal expansion, viscosity, and surface tension of molten oxides were measured using the Electrostatic Levitation Furnace (ELF) onboard the ISS. Measurements included silhouette imaging and drop oscillation over a wide range of temperatures, including deep supercooling. Recovered samples included glasses and polycrystalline materials, which were characterized post-flight using a combination of X-ray scattering, neutron scattering, X-ray tomography, Raman spectroscopy, calorimetry, and electron microscopy. Space-made glasses were compared with terrestrial analogues to investigate how changes in fluid transport may influence structure and properties. Hypothesis Glasses are inherently non-equilibrium materials. Their structure and properties depend directly on the processing route used to form them. Because gravity affects fluid convection, glass formation and resulting structure may differ between different gravity conditions. We hypothesize that the superior control of fluid and thermal transport possible in microgravity will enable improved glass forming ability via melt quenching in containerless conditions, particularly for highly fragile liquid oxides. We hypothesize that the differences in thermal history - between microgravity and Earth-based processing - will manifest as differences in structure and thermodynamic properties. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits This project advances the understanding of oxide melt properties, atomic structure, and glass formation. This builds a foundation for the development of new functional glasses and related technologies, with applications on Earth and in space for exploration and utilization of local resources. The project was successful on several fronts: 1. Microgravity experiments enabled melting, drop imaging, drop oscillation and measurements of properties of approximately 30 samples. 2. First experiments in ELF that used melt processing to make and recover glass in space. 3. First publications that compare the structure of glasses made in microgravity vs. terrestrially. 4. First demonstration of laser action in a titanate-network glass and of a glass made by containerless processing. 5. Results were analyzed in collaboration with a variety of researchers leading to 12 peer-reviewed publications and more than 20 presentations. 6. Two patents are pending based on results of the work. These have a high impact on glass materials processing in space and development of new glass products both on-Earth and in reduced gravity. 7. The project met all of the stated goals: perform basic research in microgravity, develop applied knowledge to make new materials, build collaborations to advance the field, and establish the basis for new glass materials products made in reduced gravity. 8. The results are relevant to the applied research mission of NASA's Biological and Physical Sciences (BPS) to enable space exploration and utilize materials based on natural resources that are available in space. 9. Developed and fostered a productive international collaboration.
