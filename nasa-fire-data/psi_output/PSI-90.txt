The porous media or “packing” can be made of different shapes and materials and are used widely in chemical engineering as a means to enhance the contact between two immiscible fluid phases (e.g., liquid-gas, water-oil, etc.). Packed columns can serve as reactors, scrubbers, strippers, etc. in systems where efficient interphase contact is desired, both on Earth and in space. Approach The PBRE investigation employs an approach that combines theoretical modeling with rigorous experimental testing to achieve its objectives. Here's a breakdown of the key aspects: 1. Theoretical Modeling: Existing Models: PBRE builds upon existing models for pressure drop and flow regime transitions in packed bed reactors, particularly those developed by Motil et al. (2003) and Guo et al. (2004). Model Validation: The experiment focuses on validating these models, extending them to lower flow rates relevant to water recovery systems, and bridging them with other models. New Models: The data collected through PBRE is used to develop new models and scaling laws that accurately predict flow characteristics in 0-g and partial-g environments. 2. Experimental Setup and Procedures: Test Sections: PBRE utilizes two identical test sections, each a 60 cm long, 5 cm diameter cylindrical column. One section is packed with glass beads (wetting), the other with Teflon beads (non-wetting) to isolate surface tension effects. Fluid Flow: The experiment uses nitrogen and water as the gas and liquid phases, with a wide range of flow rates, from very low to high, to cover operational regimes. Instrumentation: A comprehensive suite of sensors is used to measure flow rates, temperatures, and pressures. The experiment also includes two high-speed video cameras to visualize flow patterns. Test Matrix: The investigation is organized into several test series (''Ops''), each focused on specific flow conditions and packing materials. Data Acquisition: The experiment meticulously collects data, with careful time-correlation of measurements to ensure accurate analysis. 3. Addressing Anomalies: Flow Rate Control: The team encountered anomalies with the GN2 flow rate and pressure fluctuations, leading to troubleshooting and adjustments in the experiment's setup and procedures. High-Speed Video: The cameras experienced limitations with operating at the desired frame rates, requiring modifications to data acquisition strategies. 4. Data Analysis: Model Validation: Data is analyzed to compare experimental results with model predictions, identifying any discrepancies and refining the models. New Model Development: The collected data is used to develop new models and scaling laws, aiming for greater accuracy and applicability in microgravity environments. 5. Data Organization: Physical Sciences Informatics (PSI): The PBRE team meticulously organizes the experimental data in PSI, a database for physical sciences data, to ensure accessibility and analysis. The PBRE investigation relies on a combination of theoretical modeling, rigorous experimental testing, and comprehensive data analysis to achieve its objectives. This approach is designed to contribute to a deeper understanding of the behavior of packed bed reactors in microgravity, leading to improved design tools and operational strategies for future space applications. 6. Dissemination of Results: The findings of the PBRE-2 investigations were disseminated through: Peer-reviewed publications: Publishing research papers in scientific journals to share the results with the broader scientific community. Presentations at conferences: Presenting the research findings at relevant scientific conferences and workshops. Reports and documentation: Preparing detailed reports and documentation of the experimental procedures, data analysis, and conclusions. Hypothesis It is hypothesized that the gravity plays a significant role in determining the flow characteristics (pressure drop, flow regime transitions, liquid holdup, and mass transfer) of gas-liquid flows through porous media. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Water-recovery systems, fuel cells and other equipment on the International Space Station use packed bed reactors, but currently none are designed to handle both liquid and gas at the same time. With improved understanding of how packed bed two-phase flow works in microgravity, scientists are be able to design more efficient, lightweight thermal management and life support systems that use less energy, benefiting the Space Station and future lunar and Mars missions. Design rules for gas-liquid flows through packed columns are well developed on Earth (i.e., for normal gravity) but lacking for reduced or zero gravity. The Packed Bed Reactor Experiment seeks to fill this knowledge gap by studying the hydrodynamics of gas-liquid flows in zero gravity through packed columns. By understanding how gravity affects gas-liquid flows through packed columns (or packed beds, as they are known in the industry) better, more predictive correlations for pressure drops and flow regime maps can be developed with the proper gravity-dependent terms included.
