Task 1: Advanced Clocks in Space and Time Transfer Task 2: Maturing and Advancing Atom Interferometer Technology for Space Task 3: Precision Atom Interferometric Measurement in Space Task 4: Strategies for the Frontier of Ultracold Atoms in Space. The Consortium is: N. P. Bigelow, M. Kasevick, W. Ketterle, M. Lukin, H. Müller, W. D. Phillips, D. Pritchard, D. Stamper-Kurn, V. Vuletic, and J. Ye. Berkeley and Stanford lead Tasks 2 and 3. MIT, U.C./JILA, and Harvard lead Task 1. U. Md., Rochester, and MIT lead Task 4. Members of the Consortium can and often will contribute to all four tasks with priorities being set by the lead institutions. Approach The Consortium for Ultracold Atoms in Space (CUAS), led by Nicholas Bigelow, conducted groundbreaking experiments in atomic clocks, atom interferometry, and quantum gases, with a strong focus on the application of these quantum technologies in space environments. The consortium's research was guided by the objectives outlined in the National Research Council (NRC) report and NASA's Research Announcement (NRA), particularly in precision measurement and quantum gas studies. The Consortium fostered a collaborative approach, bringing together scientists from top institutions like MIT, Stanford, Berkeley, and Harvard, as well as international partners such as those from the DLR (German Space Agency). This collaboration allowed the group to pool expertise, regularly refine their goals, and push forward only the most promising space experiments. The procedure for the creation of an ultracold gas is fairly standardized. The atoms are released into a vacuum chamber, collected into a magneto-optical trap (MOT), and cooled via laser cooling. The sample is then optically pumped to a low magnetic field seeking quantum state and transfer from the MOT to an atom-chip based trap. The atoms are then evaporatively cooled to below the critical temperature so that a Bose-Einstein Condensate (BEC) begins to form. The atom trap is slightly relaxed to further cool the atoms, and eventually the atoms are completely released from the trap and allowed to expand freely. At this point, the ultracold gas is probed using laser, radiofrequency, and/or microwave pulses. After a prespecified time of flight, the sample is imaged using absorption imaging techniques. This is the end of the experimental cycle for this specific BEC because the measurement process destroys the sample. A total of two images are taken, the first being an image of the sample and the second being a reference image that is taken after the destructive absorption imaging process. This general process was followed by the Consortium for experiments requiring the production or probing of ultracold quantum gases. In their work on atom interferometry, the Consortium conducted experiments to detect dark-energy scalar fields with unprecedented sensitivity. The team also focused on measuring the fine structure constant with high accuracy, which involved overcoming systematic effects that could be amplified in the space environment. Collaborations with the Bose-Einstein Condensate and Cold Atom Laboratory (BECCAL) science definition team and other international partners were essential for demonstrating long coherence times in microgravity, testing the equivalence principle, and searching for dark-energy candidates. These experiments were conducted with a focus on utilizing the microgravity conditions available aboard the International Space Station (ISS) and other space-based platforms, which provided a unique environment to achieve results unattainable on Earth. In the realm of atomic clocks and quantum sensors, the Consortium developed new configurations for optical lattice clocks and achieved precision at the 19th decimal place, setting a new record for stable laser performance with a linewidth of 10 mHz. These experiments were designed to improve the accuracy of space-based timekeeping and navigation systems, which are critical for the success of space missions. The Consortium also explored innovative cooling methods, such as direct optical cooling to achieve Bose-Einstein condensation, which simplified the setup for quantum experiments in space. These methods were tested and refined in microgravity environments, demonstrating their potential to enhance the performance of quantum technologies in space. Overall, the Consortium’s approach to conducting experiments was characterized by a strong emphasis on leveraging the unique conditions of space, particularly microgravity, to achieve results that would be impossible on Earth. Their collaborative efforts, cutting-edge experimental designs, and focus on precision allowed them to make significant contributions to the field of quantum technologies in space, laying the groundwork for future space missions and advancing our understanding of fundamental physics. Hypothesis This consortium aims to provide a space for the world’s experts on Bose-Einstein Condensates (BEC), atom optics, atom interferometry, and related quantum realms to push the current boundaries of quantum gas and atomic physics space experiments. It is hypothesized that with the realization of a space atom laser and precision atomic interferometry there is potential for significant scientific advancement by testing the equivalence principle with quantum test masses and pushing the limits of the standard model. A space atom laser is expected to provide truly novel investigations of the wave-like nature of matter. Varied experiments and manipulation of quantum gases (both single-species and dual-species) including deep cooling, condensate mixture phases, and precision motional control are hypothesized to mature the technology of quantum gases and provide first of its kind opportunity to investigate interacting quantum gases in space. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Significant progress has been made on atomic interferometry and atomic clocks in terrestrial experiments. The work has long-term impact for fundamental science, navigation technologies, and global clock synchronization. Clocks are vital to navigation, communication, and security. ATOM INTERFEROMETRY We have pushed atom interferometry for space applications forward in many ways: 1. We have shown that atom interferometry can detect dark-energy scalar fields with unprecedented sensitivity. There is a chance to cover all the relevant parameter space so as to detect them or to rule them out once and for all. This work has been published in Science, Nature Physics, and Physical Review D. 2. We have demonstrated a theoretically predicted, but never observed, attractive force on atoms from blackbody radiation. It is an important limitation that has to be taken into account in the design of atomic-physics space missions. 3. We have pushed forward the accuracy of atom interferometry for measuring the fine structure constant, which has resulted in a measurement of this constant with an accuracy about three times better than the best previous one. The agreement of this measurement with others sets very strong constraints on hypothesized particles from the dark sector, such as dark photons. 4. We have developed atom interferometry with lithium atoms, opening up the possibility to do interferometry with a much wider class of atoms than available previously. We will continue to develop atom interferometry for demonstrating the gravitational Aharonov-Bohm effect, and measuring fundamental constants very precisely. We will also work with the Bose-Einstein Condensate Cold Atom Laboratory (BECCAL) science definition team and other teams to identify future targets for spaceborne fundamental physics. The interferometry work has several main thrusts. In the first thrust, we have been investigating how spaceborne atom interferometry can probe models for dark matter and dark energy. This has resulted in experimental demonstrations of such tests for so-called chameleon and symmetron models, and theoretical studies on how to make detailed predictions of the putative signals. In the second thrust, we have developed strategies to overcome systematic effects in atom interferometers that use Bragg diffraction, as the one planned for the Cold Atom lab. In the third, we have developed specific plans for spaceborne atom interferometry. We have collaborated with the German team Document for atom interferometry on the Cold Atom Lab and BECCAL. Plans include demonstrating long coherence times thanks to microgravity, tests of the equivalence principle, and searches for dark-energy candidates. They have also collaborated with Nan Yu (Jet Propulsion Laboratory-JPL) on a concept study QTEST (Quantum test of the Equivalence Principle and Spacer-Time) for testing the equivalence principle in space. CLOCKS AND QUANTUM SENSORS TECHNOLOGIES We have demonstrated the first direct optical cooling to Bose-Einstein condensation, without any evaporative cooling. This has promising impact of simplifying spaceborne experiments. The Bose-Einstein condensation by direct optical cooling was achieved for small ensembles of ~1000 atom. We will attempt to make substantially larger condensates with the same method by using a more powerful trapping laser. We have been working on spin squeezing in the optical-transition clock with trapped ytterbium atoms. By implementing both frequency and intensity feedback loops for the magical wavelength trap inside an optical cavity, we have now lengthened the trap lifetime for the Yb sample from 200 ms to 2 seconds. We have also started non-destructive state-dependent measurements for spin squeezing by observing the light transmitted through the cavity, and we can already more than resolve the shot-noise limit. In the Rb experiment, we are working towards using Rydberg states for increasing the light-atom interaction in cavity QED. We have frequency-stabilized the control laser coupling the P state to high-lying Rydberg states. We have demonstrated a new optical lattice clock configuration using a three-dimensional optical lattice, leading to measurement precision in the 19th decimal place. We have also demonstrated another record-breaking performance on stable lasers, with the narrowest laser linewidth at 10 mHz.
