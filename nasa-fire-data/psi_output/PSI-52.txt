Objectives included study of nucleation and growth of crystals within colloidal samples by varying the size and concentration of colloids and depletant, exploring the effect of temperature gradients, and demonstrating control of particle shape and density which is essential for creating high performance materials for 3D printing and advanced optical materials. The research goal is to produce various colloidal structures and study the dynamics of crystal nucleation and growth. The imaging goal is to observe crystallites and resolve particle centroid positions with less than 20% error. Approach The ACE-T11 investigation used 2 Sample Modules (SM), each containing 3 capillary cells for a total of 6 samples. Confocal microscopy was employed for 3D imaging of non-spherical colloids, with phases controlled by adding depletants, adjusting spatial temperature changes, and applying temperature gradients. The size and concentration of both the particles and depletants were varied across different samples. After homogenization using a magnetic stirrer, the particles were left undisturbed to crystallize. Preliminary observations were made with a 2.5X objective, followed by detailed imaging using 10X, 63X, and 100X objectives. Hypothesis Under microgravity conditions, colloidal particles would exhibit unique crystallization and self-assembly behaviors that differ from those observed under Earth's gravity. Researchers believed that without the effects of sedimentation, they would better understand the fundamental mechanisms governing colloidal phase transitions and self-organization​. This hypothesis also included the idea that controlling external factors like temperature and depletant concentration would enable precise manipulation of colloidal structures, allowing for the creation of more complex and functional materials. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Future space missions will require self-assembly and self-replication technologies for self-repairing materials and devices, essential for long-duration travel. 3D printing and additive manufacturing are crucial for creating replacement parts, with research focusing on developing particle slurries and pastes suited for both micro and regular gravity environments. This research advances soft matter science, with applications in medicine, electronics, and photonics. Self-assembly techniques will enable new devices for energy and communication, while 3D printing and additive manufacturing rely on optimizing high-density slurries and pastes with diverse mechanical properties.
