To measure the divergence of large-size fluctuations by observing and quantifying this divergence up to the limits imposed by the sample size. To quantify the amplitude and spatial extent of fluctuations by precisely measuring the mean-squared amplitude of the fluctuations as a function of both wave vector (spatial extent) and frequency (temporal dynamics). The investigation aims to rigorously test existing theoretical models of non-equilibrium fluctuations including comparing the measured amplitude and q-dependence of S(q) with predictions, both on Earth and in microgravity. Beyond the static properties (amplitude and spatial extent), the investigation also seeks to characterize the temporal dynamics of the fluctuations. Approach The GRADFLEX experiment investigated gradient-driven fluctuations in fluids using a dual approach: a 12-day microgravity flight experiment aboard the Foton-M3 satellite and complementary ground-based tests with the same apparatus. Two fluid systems (CS₂ and polystyrene/toluene) were subjected to precisely controlled temperature gradients. Quantitative shadowgraphy, a sensitive optical technique, measured both static (S(q)) and dynamic (S(q,ω)) properties of the fluctuations, with images acquired at specific rates for each. Data analysis involved Fourier transforms and spectral techniques to extract fluctuation amplitudes and dynamics and to address unexpected noise. The core of the study was a rigorous, parameter-free comparison of experimental results with theoretical predictions for both terrestrial and microgravity environments. The approach combined and refined a sophisticated design, enabling robust testing. Hypothesis In the absence of gravity, thermally-driven fluctuations in a fluid subjected to a temperature gradient will exhibit significantly enhanced amplitude and spatial extent, with their mean-squared amplitude diverging with the size of the fluctuation and exhibiting altered dynamics up to the limits imposed by the sample dimensions. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits Throughout the course of this work it has been necessary to constantly improve the experimental method of choice, namely the quantitative shadowgraph. This development has now reached the stage where it is clear that this method, if handled properly, represents a viable method of carrying out both dynamic and static light scattering at scattering angles so small as to defy the imagination of of anyone who has worked in the field. Although it is likely to take some time for the knowledge we have gained to be disseminated, there is no reason this method cannot be added to the scattering arsenal. Furthermore, it can also be applied to X-rays, enabling one to extend the lower limit of X-ray scattering as well. The consequences of putting a new technique into the hands of researchers is difficult to asses in advance. Basically, however, this method will enable quantitative measurements of various systems having size ranges that are too large for existing techniques. Obvious examples are the crystallization process in colloidal systems, gelation processes, and aggregation and flocculation. Because scattering provides excellent statistical averages, the method should prove quite complementary to visualization techniques such as microscopy. Contact(s) David S. Cannellemail,William V.
