Approach The experiment involved making a series of measurements on approximately 8 different samples of different concentrations (i.e. volume fractions of PMMA in an index matching fluid). The samples were made from a mixture of decalin, tetralin, and PMMA spheres with a stabilizing polymer grafted onto them. The decalin (index - 1.48) and the tetralin (index - 1.54) was mixed in a combination which index matches the PMMA (index - 1.51), allowing the use of single photon counting techniques to probe the particle dynamics at different concentrations. Once in orbit, the experiment was initialized and each of the samples melted to start the nucleation and growth phase. After melting, each sample was examined to ensure that complete melting has occurred, and immediate static scattering measurements will begin and progress for 24 hours on the nucleating sample. After each melting phase, a concentration dependent nucleation and growth phase occurred and was monitored by static light scattering measurements. These measurements proceeded while the experiment entered into an equilibration phase during which longer range order formed. The static light scattering measurements proceeded until the Bragg peaks were well developed and sufficient data for ensemble averages were taken for a sound statistical basis for the conclusions intended to experimentally ascertain. Periodic measurements, both static and dynamic, were made to gage the progress and determine if alternate procedures are required. During this period, dynamic light scattering measurements were performed on the nucleating sample. Other measurements such as rheology measurements were performed on the other seven samples after this 24 hours period. Hypothesis Some colloids (a system of fine particles suspended in a fluid) have the ability to act like a gas, liquid, solid, or even glass, depending on the relative concentration between the suspended material and the solution they are suspended in, and/or the presence or absence of gravity. The behavior of a densely packed colloid here on earth mimics glass in the distribution of its particles, while in space, the same density colloid acts more closely like a solid. This results in a highly organized, lattice-like arrangement of particles in the colloid. It is hypothesized that the complete set of data from the microgravity experiments to test scalings proposed in the literature, e.g. the shear rate dependence of the viscosity and the rate of crystal growth with either the short- or the long-term self diffusion coefficient; the relationships among the osmotic pressure, the static shear modulus, and the yield stress for solid phases. Research Impacts/Earth Benefits The colloidal engineering process will play a fundamental role in the creation of new materials and products in space, such as optical switches and lasers for communications and displays, and 3D Bragg crystals for controlling light. Understanding the physics describing the Packing of Hard Spheres has everyday impacts and benefits here on earth including Grain packing; Structure of Liquids, Solids and Glasses; Freezing Transition; Flow and Plasticity of Sand; Optimization of data storage and transmission in the presence of Noise and Opto-electronic Materials, Light Localization and Photonic Bandgaps. The ultimate payoff will be a definitive set of data for the hard spheres useful for (a) testing fundamental correlations among physical properties, (b) serving as a reference for understanding the behavior of systems with more complex interaction potentials, and (c) guiding attempts to control or modify the structure of densely packed media.
